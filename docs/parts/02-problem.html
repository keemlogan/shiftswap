2. Problem Statement, Background and Related Work

This chapter describes the problem that ShiftSwap addresses. Section 2.1 describes how substitutes are arranged today in the reference store and models the process as a data flow diagram. Section 2.2 states the three problems P1–P3 that the process causes. Section 2.3 summarises the legal rules that govern part-time pay in Korea and that the system must apply. Section 2.4 compares existing shift-scheduling products, and Section 2.5 identifies the gap that ShiftSwap fills.

2.1 Current Process in the Reference Store

2.1.1 The reference store

The requirements were drawn from one real workplace, a small café where Hyewon Kim works part-time. To protect the store and its staff, the report does not name the store; the demonstration data of the system uses a fictitious café, Dalbit Café (달빛카페), with one owner and four part-time workers, which has the same structure. The real store has the following characteristics, which are typical of the stores the system targets:

  • fewer than ten part-time workers; whether the store reaches five regular employees, the threshold for premium pay (Sections 2.3.3 and 2.3.5), depends on how many workers it employs at a given time, so the system treats the number as a setting;
  • the owner acts as the manager, sets the schedule and pays the wages; there is no separate HR or payroll staff;
  • each worker has a fixed weekly pattern of shifts written in the employment contract, and the pattern changes rarely;
  • the schedule is kept in a spreadsheet maintained by the owner, and messages about substitutes are sent by phone call or personal message;
  • wages are paid monthly, and the owner calculates them at the end of the month from the spreadsheet.

2.1.2 The as-is substitute process, step by step

The following description is the current procedure for one substitute request, as reported by the team member who works in the reference store. It is written as the normal sequence; the variations are listed afterwards.

  1. The worker reports the absence. A worker who cannot attend a coming shift sends the owner a personal message with the date and the reason, for example "I cannot come on Wednesday evening because of a midterm exam."
  2. The owner looks up possible substitutes. The owner opens the schedule spreadsheet, checks who is not working at that time, and recalls from memory who is usually free on that weekday. Availability is not recorded anywhere.
  3. The owner contacts workers one by one. The owner calls or messages the first person on the mental list and waits for an answer. If the person refuses or does not answer within a reasonable time, the owner contacts the next person. In the reference store this typically takes three to five contacts per request.
  4. A worker agrees. When one worker agrees, the owner confirms to that worker and to the worker who asked. The other workers who were contacted may not be told that the shift has been filled.
  5. The owner updates the spreadsheet. The owner changes the name in the cell for that shift and tells the workers concerned.
  6. The owner rechecks weekly hours at month end. At the end of the month the owner adds up each worker's hours per week from the spreadsheet, including the swapped shifts.
  7. The owner decides the holiday allowance. For each worker and week, the owner decides from memory whether the worker had at least 15 contractual hours and attended every working day, and whether a swapped shift counts as an absence.
  8. The owner calculates the monthly pay. The owner multiplies hours by the hourly wage, adds the holiday allowance, and transfers the pay. Whether the hourly wage is still above the current minimum wage is not checked systematically.

Three variations occur regularly. Workers sometimes arrange a swap between themselves and only inform the owner afterwards, or not at all, in which case the spreadsheet does not match reality. Two workers may both agree when the owner has contacted them in parallel, and one of them must then be told that the shift is no longer free. Finally, when nobody agrees, the owner covers the shift personally, and this is often not recorded.

2.1.3 Data flow diagram of the as-is process

The figure below models the procedure as a data flow diagram (DFD) following the rules of Chapter 2 of the lecture [6]: boxes are processes named with verb phrases, arcs are data named with noun phrases, control is not shown, and actors are not included. Arcs that enter from outside the diagram carry data supplied by a person (the worker or the owner) whose identity is omitted by the DFD rules. Two outputs from one box mean that one or the other is produced: process 3 produces either a refusal, which feeds process 2 again, or a substitute agreement.

The diagram makes two properties of the current process visible. First, the loop between processes 2.0 and 3.0 carries one inquiry and one refusal at a time; the number of iterations of this loop is the cost described as problem P1. Second, processes 5.0 to 7.0 depend on data that is not recorded in the store: "time records" are notes kept by the owner, and "remembered legal rules" is literally the owner's memory. These two inputs are the source of problems P2 and P3.

2.2 Problems of the Current Process

The as-is process causes three problems. Each problem statement below gives the observable symptom, the cause in the data flow diagram of Section 2.1.3, and the consequence.

P1. Finding a substitute costs repeated one-to-one contacts

For every substitute request the owner contacts workers one at a time, typically three to five contacts per request in the reference store. The cause is that the information needed to choose a candidate — who is free at that time and who is willing to work extra — exists only in the owner's memory and in the workers' heads, so the owner has to ask. Because each inquiry waits for an answer, the elapsed time grows with the number of contacts, and a request made late in the evening may not be settled until the next day. When the owner contacts several people in parallel to save time, two of them may agree, and one has to be disappointed. The workers who refused or were not asked do not see whether the shift has been filled.

P2. Every swap changes the hours of two workers, and all recalculation is manual

A swap removes a shift from one worker's week and adds it to another's. Both workers' weekly scheduled hours change, and the holiday-allowance eligibility of either of them may change depending on how the missed shift is counted. In the current process the spreadsheet cell is edited (process 4.0), but the effect on hours is not calculated until the end of the month (process 5.0), when the owner must reconstruct from the edited spreadsheet which shifts were swapped and when. The owner approves a swap without seeing its effect on pay, and if the spreadsheet was not updated for a swap arranged directly between workers, the monthly calculation is wrong.

P3. Legal pay rules are applied from memory, so errors are not caught before payday

The weekly holiday allowance, the minimum wage, the five-employee threshold for premium pay, the limit on work of part-time workers beyond their contractual hours and the probation reduction are all conditional rules with numeric thresholds (Section 2.3). In the reference store they are applied from memory at month end. Errors that this practice allows include paying a holiday allowance to a worker below 15 contractual hours, not paying it to a worker at or above the threshold, continuing to pay the previous year's minimum wage after 1 January, applying the 90 % probation rate to a worker whose contract does not qualify for it, and, in a store with five or more regular employees, approving a swap that takes a part-time worker more than 12 hours beyond the contractual hours of the week. Because no check is made before the transfer, these errors are discovered, if at all, only when a worker complains.

2.3 Legal Background

Part-time workers in Korea are covered by the same labour statutes as full-time workers and, in addition, by the Act on the Protection, etc. of Fixed-term and Part-time Employees (in this report, the Fixed-term and Part-time Employees Act). The provisions below determine the pay rules the system implements as business rules BR-06 to BR-10 and BR-15 (Chapter 3). The team read the statutes on the National Law Information Center [3], [4], [15] and the explanations of the government's legal information service for part-time workers [5]. The system is a tool for applying these rules consistently; it is not legal advice, and the rules were simplified in the ways stated below. Where the law leaves the method open, the system's rule is stated as a design decision of this project (Section 2.3.5).

2.3.1 Minimum wage

Under the Minimum Wage Act, the Minister of Employment and Labor announces the minimum hourly wage for each year. For 2026 the minimum hourly wage is KRW 10,320, up from KRW 10,030 in 2025, an increase of about 2.9 % [1], [2]. An employer who pays less than the minimum wage breaks the law regardless of what the contract says. For the system this means that the applicable minimum depends on the year of the pay period, so the minimum wage is stored as a table of yearly values (table MinimumWage, UC-10) rather than as a constant, and each payroll row is checked against the value for its year (BR-09).

2.3.2 Weekly paid holiday and the 15-hour threshold

Article 55 ① of the Labor Standards Act requires an employer to give a worker at least one paid holiday per week on average. Article 30 ① of the Enforcement Decree specifies that this paid holiday is given to a worker who has attended all the working days agreed for that week. Article 18 ③ excludes from this provision workers whose contractual working hours, averaged over four weeks, are less than 15 hours per week [3]. For a part-time worker the paid holiday is proportional to the worker's hours; the government's guidance computes the paid holiday hours as weekly contractual hours ÷ 40 × 8, with the weekly hours capped at 40 [5]. The money paid for this holiday is what Korean workers call 주휴수당, rendered in this report as the weekly holiday allowance.

Two points required interpretation. The first is the four-week average. In the target stores a worker's contractual hours come from a fixed weekly schedule, which is the same every week; the four-week average therefore equals the weekly value, and the system uses the weekly contractual hours directly (BR-06). The second is a swapped shift. When a worker gives a shift to a colleague with the owner's approval, the working day has been changed by agreement, and the team's reading of [5] is that this should not count as a failure to attend. Because store practice differs, the system makes this a workplace setting (subAttendancePolicy = EXCUSED, the default, or ABSENT) rather than hard-coding one interpretation.

2.3.3 Premium pay and the five-employee threshold

Article 56 of the Labor Standards Act requires an additional 50 % of the ordinary wage for extended work (beyond 8 hours a day or 40 hours a week) and for night work between 22:00 and 06:00. Article 11 limits the application of the Act: a workplace that ordinarily employs fewer than five workers is subject only to the provisions listed in the Enforcement Decree, and Article 56 is not among them [3]. Many small cafés fall below this threshold; the demonstration store Dalbit Café is configured with four regular employees. The system therefore stores the number of regular employees as a workplace setting and computes premium pay only when it is five or more (BR-08). Holiday-work premium under Article 56 ② is outside the scope of the system. For part-time workers, the same threshold also governs the premium for hours beyond the contractual hours (Section 2.3.5).

2.3.4 Probation reduction

Article 5 ② of the Minimum Wage Act allows an employer to pay 90 % of the minimum wage during the first three months of employment for a worker under probation, but only if the employment contract is for one year or more, and not for workers in simple labour occupations designated by the Ministry of Employment and Labor (Enforcement Decree Art. 3) [4]. Many part-time jobs in cafés and convenience stores, such as serving and cashier work, fall into the designated simple labour occupations. The system requires all conditions to hold before it lowers the minimum-wage threshold for a worker (BR-10).

2.3.5 Work of part-time workers beyond contractual hours

The Fixed-term and Part-time Employees Act adds a rule that concerns substitutes directly. Like the premiums of Section 2.3.3, it applies only in workplaces with five or more regular employees (Art. 3 ②; Enforcement Decree Art. 2 and Annex 1). In such workplaces a part-time worker may work beyond their contractual hours only with their consent and for at most 12 hours a week, and these hours earn a premium of at least 50 % (Art. 6) [15]. Accepting a substitute shift usually creates such hours, so the rule bears on the owner's decision to approve a swap.

For counting these hours the system applies the following rules. They are design decisions of this project, taken where the law does not fix the method, and are not statements of law (BR-08 (iv) with its rules (a)–(e), and BR-15, in Chapter 3):

  • a worker whose weekly contractual hours C, the sum of the fixed schedule, satisfy 0 < C < 40 is treated as part-time; a worker without a fixed schedule (C = 0) or with C ≥ 40 is not;
  • the test is weekly against C, not daily: the hours of an ISO week are taken in chronological order, and every hour after the first C hours is beyond the contract;
  • an hour that is both daily or weekly overtime (Section 2.3.3) and beyond the contract is counted once, and the night premium is added separately;
  • the worker's consent is outside the system, and the 12-hour weekly limit is checked before approval: the approval panel warns when a swap would leave a part-time acceptor with more than 12 hours beyond the contractual hours in that week, without blocking the decision (BR-15).

Two other readings were considered and not adopted: a daily test against daily contractual hours, and the definition of part-time by comparison with a full-time worker doing the same kind of work in the same workplace. Both need data the system does not keep.

2.4 Related Work

Shift scheduling is an established product category. The team compared seven products on the basis of their own help pages and public descriptions, consulted on 29 September 2026: three international products, Deputy, When I Work and Homebase, and four Korean products, Shiftee, Shopl, Albami and Gig. The comparison records only what these pages state.

Scheduling products abroad already support a substitute workflow. In Deputy a worker can offer a shift to co-workers who are qualified and available; the manager is notified when the offer is accepted, and swaps can be set to require the manager's approval [10]. When I Work lets a worker offer a shift to all or selected eligible co-workers, subject by default to a manager's approval, and its Find Replacement function lets a manager offer a shift to eligible workers, and the first one to pick it up takes it [18], [19]. In Homebase a worker asks all or selected co-workers to cover a shift, and a manager gives final approval after one of them accepts [20]. Adopting these products in a small Korean store is not straightforward: Deputy's employee app is available in Korean, but managers who build schedules on its web interface must work in English [21], and the team found no mention of the Korean weekly holiday allowance in the help centres of the three products.

Korean products follow a different workflow, in which the manager edits the schedule or an employee sends a change request to the manager. Shiftee lets an employee request a new or changed shift, tags the request when it involves overtime, night or holiday work, and computes the weekly holiday allowance automatically [22], [23]. Its help centre states that the allowance is recognised only when every contractual working day is worked, so that a worker contracted for Monday to Friday who works Tuesday to Saturday is not eligible [23]. Shopl lets employees request schedule changes that the manager approves or rejects [24]. Albami lets the owner manage recurring and daily schedules and set the holiday allowance for each worker [25], and Gig shares the owner's schedule with the workers and manages allowances including the holiday allowance [26]. The public feature descriptions of these four products do not include a substitute request between co-workers.

The team found no product that combines the co-worker substitute request with the Korean holiday-allowance rule, that is, one that shows the owner at the moment of approval how a swap changes the holiday-allowance eligibility of both workers. ShiftSwap adopts the substitute workflow that Deputy, When I Work and Homebase already offer and applies the Korean pay rules to it. Because a strict every-contract-day rule would also remove the allowance of a worker who hands a shift to a colleague, ShiftSwap makes the treatment of such a shift a workplace setting (Section 2.3.2, BR-06).

2.5 The Gap ShiftSwap Fills

ShiftSwap targets the part of the problem that the products compared in Section 2.4 do not address for small Korean stores. Its contribution is the combination of four elements in one workflow:

  1. candidate selection from the existing shifts, so that a request reaches at once every co-worker who is not already working at that time, and ends with a clear message to the owner when none of them can take it (addresses P1). The proposal planned selection by recorded availability; this was replaced in iteration 6 (Section 3.11.4);
  2. a first-acceptance-wins rule enforced in a single database transaction, so that two acceptances can never both succeed; in the shared database the server applies each change under a lock on the store and refuses a change computed on outdated data (addresses the double-acceptance variation of P1);
  3. an approval step that shows, for both workers, contractual hours, scheduled hours and holiday-allowance eligibility before and after the swap, and recomputes the weekly summaries on approval (addresses P2);
  4. pay rules taken from Korean law, including the premium for hours of part-time workers beyond their contractual hours, and stored as data where they change (minimum wage by year, number of regular employees, substitution-attendance policy), with warnings before payroll confirmation and, when a swap would leave a part-time acceptor with more than 12 hours beyond the contractual hours of the week, before approval (addresses P3).

The system is deliberately small. It serves one demonstration store, whose data are kept in a hosted shared database that the owner and every worker reach from their own phones; it has no application server of its own, keeps a local demonstration mode in the browser as a fallback, and does not replace a full payroll or HR system. Its value lies in making the rules explicit and applying them at the moment a decision is made, rather than at the end of the month. Chapter 3 turns these four elements into use cases, functional requirements and business rules.
